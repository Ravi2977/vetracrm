Expired medicines are one of the biggest hidden losses in a medical store. Stock sits at the back of a shelf, a new batch arrives in front of it, and months later someone discovers strips that can no longer be sold.

The good news is that expiry losses are very controllable. Here is a routine that works.

1. Record expiry for every batch at purchase

Expiry control starts at purchase entry. If the batch and expiry date are not recorded when stock comes in, no report can help later. Make it a rule: no purchase bill is saved without batch and expiry.

2. Sell first-expiry, first-out (FEFO)

Arrange shelves so earlier-expiry stock is in front, and use software that picks the nearest-expiry batch automatically at billing. This one habit prevents most expiry.

3. Review near-expiry stock monthly

Once a month, pull a report of items expiring in the next 60–90 days. For each item, decide:

  • Can it sell in time at normal pace?
  • Should it move to the front shelf or another branch?
  • Should it be returned or exchanged with the distributor, as per their policy?

4. Buy based on real sales

Over-buying slow-moving medicines is the root cause of expiry. Use sales history to set minimum and maximum stock levels. Be careful with large-quantity schemes on slow items: the free goods are only free if they sell before expiry.

5. Track returns and credit notes

Record expiry and breakage returns to suppliers and follow up on credit notes. Untracked returns are money you may never receive.

Example

A chemist runs the 90-day expiry report on the 1st of every month. In the first month, she finds ₹14,000 of stock expiring soon, returns ₹6,000 worth as per distributor terms, moves the rest to the front counter, and reduces the order quantity for those items.

6. Measure expiry loss

Track the value of stock written off for expiry every month. When you can see the number, you can reduce it.
